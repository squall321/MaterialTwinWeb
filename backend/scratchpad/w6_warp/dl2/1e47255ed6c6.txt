Introduction


1.1     Background on foam materials and constitutive mod-

        eling

Elastomeric foams are materials that consist of two phases: an elastomeric matrix and air.
The mechanical behavior of the foam is determined by its microstructure and the material
properties of its matrix material. Elastomeric foams exhibit mechanical behavior marked by
high compressibility and strong coupling between the volumetric and distortional responses.
Moreover, due to the viscoelasticity of the matrix material, these materials are usually
able to absorb greater amounts of energy than ordinary stiff elastic materials for a certain
stress-level and strain-rate. They are commonly used in protective equipment, mattresses,
cushions, padding, and packaging materials that benefit from energy absorption capabilities.

   The wide applications necessitate an appropriate constitutive model for this class of
materials. One major aim of research on elastomeric foams is to develop a hyperelastic
model for their equilibrium, elastic response. There are several modeling approaches for
the free energy density function: phenomenological curve-fitting approaches that depend
on the principal stretches [1–7], Blatz-Ko type hyperelasticity models [8–10] that are based
on the principal invariants of the Cauchy-Green deformation tensor, homogenization-based
                                             1
2
approaches [11–14] that consider porous elastomers with microstructures consisting of
closed-cell pores, fully-micromechanical models based on strut-type unit cells [15–19], or
unit cells involving cylindrical or spherical voids [14, 20, 21].

    Researchers have also considered modeling the rate-dependent mechanical behavior
of elastomeric foams, in which the situation is yet more complex. Most existing finite
viscoelasticity models decompose the response into a time-independent hyperelastic part
and a time-dependent viscoelastic part. The time-dependent part of finite viscoelasticity
models for elastomeric foams are typically formulated using one of two approaches. The first
approach is a hereditary integral model, which is motivated by linear viscoelastic models.
Hereditary integral models are formulated in terms of relaxation or memory functions
[e.g., 5, 22–26]. The second approach is based on the framework of the multiplicative
decomposition of the deformation gradient into elastic and inelastic parts [e.g., 27–33].
Few researchers have reported on the modelling of compressible materials [e.g., 34], and
most works deal with incompressible or nearly-incompressible materials. Compared to
hereditary integral models, the multiplicative decomposition of the deformation gradient
leads to models that are thermodynamic consistent and more straightforwardly applied to
arbitrary three-dimensional loading histories.

    Existing models can capture certain features of the response of viscoelastic foams but
are often only applicable to specific loading conditions, e.g., simple compression or only the
loading portion of the response. Developing an effective constitutive model that remains
predictive for general deformation histories remains challenging despite the ubiquity of
these materials.




1.2     Thesis contributions

The overarching motivation of this thesis is to develop a more general and more accessible
constitutive modeling framework for elastomeric foam materials. The specific problems
                                                                                           3
addressed by this thesis are summarized as follows.


1.2.1    Hyperelastic constitutive modeling of elastomeric foams

We propose a methodology for the constitutive modeling of non-localizing, isotropic, open-
cell elastomeric foam materials under quasi-static, equilibrium loading. Using data from
large-deformation, homogeneous simple compression/tension experiments on three rela-
tive densities of a polyurethane-based elastomeric foam, we propose a phenomenological,
isotropic, hyperelastic constitutive model. The model is based on the invariants of the
logarithmic strain and accounts for high compressibility and strong volumetric-distortional
coupling. To validate the predictive capability of the model, we consider three types of
validation experiments that involve inhomogeneous deformation: spherical and conical
indentation, simple-shear-like deformation both without and with a fixed amount of pre-
compression, and tension of a specimen with circular holes. We compare load-displacement
responses as well as full displacement fields from the validation experiments against corre-
sponding model predictions obtained using finite-element-based numerical simulations and
demonstrate that the model is capable of accurately capturing the experimental response.


1.2.2    Nonlinear constitutive modeling of viscoelastic foams

We propose a methodology for the constitutive modeling of the time-dependent, large-
deformation mechanical behavior of isotropic, viscoelastic foam materials under quasi-static
and moderate rate loading. The phenomenological modeling framework is motivated by the
rheological concept of a “hyperelastic spring” in parallel with several nonlinear Maxwell
elements and consists of a time-independent, hyperelastic contribution and several time-
dependent, viscoelastic contributions. The hyperelastic modeling is based the contribution
described in Section 1.2.1, and for the rate-dependent mechanisms, we employ the frame-
work of the multiplicative decomposition of the deformation gradient into non-equilibrium
elastic and viscous parts.
4
1.2.3    Applications to closed-cell and open-cell foams

Data from large-deformation, homogeneous simple compression/tension experiments under
quasi-static and moderate strain-rates for both closed-cell and open-cell elastomeric foams
is used to specialize and calibrate the nonlinear, viscoelastic constitutive model. Validation
experiments that involve inhomogeneous deformation and a wide range of deformation
rates are conducted, which include spherical indentation, simple-shear-like deformation
both without and with a fixed amount of pre-compression, and tension of a specimen with
circular holes at different deformation rates. The responses from the validation experiments
are compared against model predictions obtained from finite-element-based numerical
simulations. We show that the experimental response can be accurately captured by the
proposed model for both closed-cell and open-cell foam materials.



1.3     Thesis outline

This thesis is outlined around the above three contributions. Chapter 2 presents our method-
ology for the hyperelastic constitutive modeling of non-localizing, isotropic elastomeric
foam materials under quasi-static, equilibrium loading and its application to an open-cell
foam material. Chapter 3 presents our methodology for viscoelastic constitutive modeling
of non-localizing, isotropic elastomeric foam materials under quasi-static and moderate rate
loading. Chapter 4 and Chapter 5 describe the applications of the proposed viscoelastic
model to a closed-cell foam and open-cell foams, respectively. We close in Chapter 6 with a
summary of the major contributions of the thesis and future research directions.

2.1     Introduction

Open-cell elastomeric foams are materials consisting of two continuous phases: a polymeric
elastomer and a connected gas-filled pore space. Due to the elastomeric matrix and porous
structure, these materials are highly compliant and capable of undergoing large, reversible
deformations involving substantial volume change. Elastomeric foams are widely used
in situations in which compliance and compressibility are necessary, such as in impact
                                            5
6
protection and cushioning, and appear in a wide variety of applications.

    The mechanical behavior of open-cell elastomeric foams is quite complex, involving
large deformations, substantial compressibility, and strong tension/compression asymmetry.
Consider, as an illustrative example, the behavior of a commercially-available impact protec-
tion foam – a polyurethane-based, open-cell elastomeric foam that will be the focus of our
work – whose response to external load is demonstrated in Fig. 2.1 for three different relative
densities (Poron XRD, Rogers Corp, Rogers, CT). For each density, Fig. 2.1 shows the
experimentally-measured engineering stress versus axial engineering strain response in the
left column and the corresponding lateral engineering strain versus axial engineering strain
response in the right column for quasi-static (low strain-rate) simple compression/tension
(experimental details are discussed in Section 2.2). The response in all three cases is highly
nonlinear and displays substantial asymmetry between tension and compression. In par-
ticular, in compression, the response is initially linear, but as the axial strain magnitude
increases, the response gradually transitions into a “plateau” in which both the axial stress
and the lateral strain vary minimally as the axial strain magnitude increases. The plateau
regime is generally associated with buckling in the elastomeric-matrix microstructure and
concomitant closure of the pore space [35]. Importantly, the plateau regime for Poron XRD
is not completely flat, and we observe homogeneous deformation with no evidence of strain
localization in the compression experiments. Then, as the material is compressed beyond the
plateau regime, rapid stiffening is observed in the axial stress response. However, in tension
neither a plateau regime nor a rapidly stiffening response is observed. Upon unloading in
both compression and tension, some hysteresis but no permanent set is observed. This is in
contrast to the inelastic behavior of crushable foams [e.g., 36–39], which yield and display
permanent set upon unloading. In this work, we focus on the mechanics of elastic foams
that exhibit neither deformation localization nor permanent set.

developing predictive constitutive models remains challenging despite the ubiquity of

foam materials [e.g., 35, 40]. Here, we focus on hyperelastic modeling approaches for
capturing the equilibrium, elastic response described in the preceding paragraph. Commonly,
Ogden-type hyperelastic models [1, 2], which utilize a phenomenological free-energy
density function that depends on the principal stretches, are used to capture data for highly-
compressible foams in the large-stretch regime [e.g., 3–7]. While models of this type are
capable of describing the features of the equilibrium, hyperelastic response of open-cell
elastomeric foams, they can be unwieldy to work with [e.g., 41, 42]. Material parameter
fitting is a challenge, and a fitted model typically has minimal extrapolative potential, often
providing poor predictions in loading scenarios not used in fitting or becoming unstable
beyond the fitted range. Other hyperelasticity models utilize free-energy density functions
that depend on the principal invariants of the Cauchy-Green deformation tensors, including
the well-known Blatz-Ko hyperelasticity model [8] and its extensions [e.g., 9, 10]. The
Blatz-Ko model can capture the response of compressible foams up to moderate stretches
but is not equipped to describe large-stretch behavior – specifically, the plateau regime
in compression. Other invariant-based approaches include polynomial-type free-energy
density functions [22, 24]. Furthermore, homogenization-based approaches [11–14] consider
porous elastomers with microstructures consisting of closed-cell pores and result in explicit,
invariant-based hyperelastic models that incorporate the effect of the void volume fraction.
However, since these models invoke closed-cell microstructures in their derivations and
do not account for buckling in elastomeric-matrix ligaments, they cannot be expected to
capture the response of highly-compressible, open-cell foams under large compressive
deformations – in particular, the response in the plateau regime. Finally, we note that
fully-micromechanical models, based on strut-type unit cells [e.g., 15–19] or unit cells
involving cylindrical or spherical voids [e.g., 14, 20, 21], have been used to capture the
precise behavior of elastomeric foams with different microstructures. While such modeling
approaches provide important physical insights, they are computationally expensive to apply
                                                                                            9
to problems involving length scales much greater than the characteristic size of the porous
microstructure, and hence, explicit hyperelastic models are valuable.


   The purpose of this chapter is to present a comprehensive methodology for the experi-
mental characterization and constitutive modeling of non-localizing, isotropic, open-cell
elastomeric foam materials under quasi-static, equilibrium loading. Initial model develop-
ment and material parameter estimation are informed by the homogeneous simple compres-
sion/tension experiments shown in Fig. 2.1. Accurate full-field kinematic measurements
in these experiments are obtained using the quality-factor-based digital image correlation
(qDIC) procedure developed in the work of Landauer et al. [43], which is crucial to measure
both axial and lateral strains under large deformations. Rather than basing our isotropic
hyperelasticity model on the principal invariants of the Cauchy-Green deformation tensors,
our hyperelastic model adopts those of Criscione et al. [44], which are based on the invari-
ants of the logarithmic strain [45, 46]. This choice is made on pragmatic grounds, since
the logarithmic-strain invariants of Criscione et al. [44] may be associated with specific
aspects of deformation, enabling a more straightforward path to choosing and calibrating
phenomenological fitting functions that capture experimental data. For perspective, a num-
ber of isotropic, logarithmic-strain-based hyperelasticity models have been proposed in
the literature. Importantly, the work of Anand [47, 48], showed that a simple quadratic
free-energy function employing the logarithmic strain is capable of capturing experimental
data for a wide class of materials in the moderate-stretch regime. Since then, various models
have been successfully applied to incompressible and nearly-incompressible materials in the
large-stretch regime [e.g., 49–52]. The present work extends this literature towards a consti-
tutive modeling approach for highly-compressible materials. We decompose the free-energy
function into terms that represent the coupled volumetric/distortional, purely distortional,
and purely volumetric contributions to the response and select phenomenological fitting
functions for each term that capture the experimental data for open-cell polyurethane foam.
We then perform several validation tests that probe the predictive capability of the model
10
in a variety of deformation modes, namely, compression, shear, and tension-dominated
situations. Our intent is to provide an accessible characterization and modeling framework
for highly-compressible elastic materials, and we expect that future works will consider
other specific, perhaps micromechanically-based, fitting functions and extend the approach
to other open-cell elastomeric foam materials.

     The remainder of this chapter is organized as follows. In Section 2.2, we describe
the experimental program for characterizing the mechanical response of the isotropic,
polyurethane-based elastomeric foam, marketed under the “Poron XRD” trademark, using
homogeneous simple compression/tension experiments as well as the approach for validation
testing. Then, in Section 2.3, we present our hyperelastic constitutive model for the elastic
behavior of isotropic, open-cell elastomeric foams and apply the model to Poron XRD. In
Section 2.4, the constitutive model is validated in three configurations: first, spherical and
conical indentation, second, simple-shear-like deformation both without and with fixed-
displacement pre-compression, and third, tension of a specimen with circular holes, where
the conditions are specifically chosen to probe the model in compression, shear, and tension-
dominated situations. In Section 2.5, we discuss issues of stability and compare the proposed
model with other important models from the literature. Finally, we close with a summary
and a discussion of the limits of the modeling approach and future research directions in
Section 2.6.




2.2      Experimental Program

Note: The experiments described in this section as well as the experiments described in
Section 2.4 were performed by Alexander K. Landauer. We include this section here for
completeness. Additional experimental detail may be found in Landauer [53].

     In this section, we describe the process for experimentally testing the mechanical
response of isotropic, elastomeric foams. We perform two classes of experiments: (1)
                                                                                             11
homogeneous characterization experiments that are utilized to choose constitutive equations
and estimate material parameters and (2) inhomogeneous validation experiments that are
utilized to test constitutive model predictions. First, regarding characterization testing, we
perform simple compression and simple tension experiments. All kinematic data in these
experiments are obtained using full-field digital image correlation (DIC), which enables both
axial and lateral strains to be obtained. Moreover, we are able to ensure that no unintended
inhomogeneous deformation (e.g., specimen barreling or buckling, compaction banding,
surface wrinkling, etc.) occurs during characterization experiments. Second, we perform a
variety of inhomogeneous validation tests, namely, spherical and conical indentation, simple-
shear-like deformation both without and with pre-compression, and tension of a specimen
with circular holes, each of which are discussed further in Section 2.4. These validation
experiments probe the predictive capability of the constitutive model in compression, shear,
and tension-dominated situations.




Foam material      The experimental testing procedures and constitutive theory presented in
this chapter are intended to be applicable to a large class of isotropic, open-cell elastomeric
foams, provided that the material is capable of deforming homogeneously and does not
exhibit localized deformation, such as compaction banding. In the present work, we
consider three different relative densities of a single type of foam material. Our selection is
a polyurethane-based open-cell elastomeric foam developed by Rogers Corporation (Poron

Specimen preparation      The details of specimen preparation depend on the specific type
of test, but in general, specimens are collected from as-received approximately 12.9 mm-

in appropriate fixtures. For compression testing, foam cuboids of approximately 10 ×
10 × 12.9 mm3 are excised with a bandsaw. For tension testing, dogbone specimens are
cut using a 75W CO2 laser cutter (Universal Laser Systems Inc., Scottsdale, AZ) to the
ASTM D3574 standard geometry for tensile testing of foam polymers. For indentation
experiments (Section 2.4.1), 25.4 × 25.4 × 12.9 mm3 samples are laser cut and bonded
to an aluminum backer on one of the large faces using a two-part epoxy (ITW Polymers
Adhesives, Danvers, MA). For each simple-shear-like experiment (Section 2.4.2), two
30 × 30 × 12.9 mm3 specimens are laser cut, and each large, square face is epoxied to
an aluminum backer. Finally, circular holes are cut out of tension specimens to be used
for the third type of validation testing (Section 2.4.3). DIC measurements are used to
obtain kinematic data in all experiments except for indentation testing, and to prepare
specimens for DIC analysis, a face orthogonal to the testing axis is speckle-painted for
each specimen. To speckle the specimens, a black base coat of acrylic ink (Liquitex Artist
                                                                                           13
Materials, Piscataway, NJ) is airbrushed (HP-C Plus, Iwata Medea Inc, Portland, OR) on
the surface, followed by a white speckle layer (Golden Artist Colors Inc., New Berlin, NY).
For each configuration, the optical train employed is configured such that this technique
yields speckles of approximately 3 to 7 pixels in size. Due to the inherent DIC challenges
posed by mechanical testing of elastomeric foams – namely, that large speckle distortions
and speckle loss due to cell collapse or opening arise when the material undergoes finite
deformations – a custom, DIC procedure is utilized [43], which incorporates the concept of
cross-correlation quality factors to improve the accuracy of DIC measurements involving
distorted speckle patterns undergoing large deformations. Finally, for all experiments, both
characterization and validation, three specimens are tested, and each specimen is tested three
times (i.e., nine total experiments per condition). For repeated experiments on the same
specimen, we ensure that the wait-time between experiments is sufficiently long to allow
complete relaxation of any time-dependent material physics, typically 3 hours or more in
total test time.

2.3        Constitutive theory

In this section, we describe our hyperelastic constitutive model for the elastic behavior of
isotropic, elastomeric foams and apply the model to three densities of Poron XRD foam.



Basic kinematics         We consider a body B identified with the region of space it occupies in
a fixed reference configuration and denote an arbitrary material point within B as X. The
referential body B then undergoes a motion x = χ(X,t) to the deformed body Bt at each
time t. The deformation gradient is given by F = ∇χ, such that the ratio of referential
to deformed volume is strictly greater than zero, i.e., J = det F > 0.2 The displacement
field is denoted as u(X,t) = χ(X,t) − X, and the left and right Cauchy-Green deformation
tensors are B = FF> and C = F> F, respectively. The deformation tensors admit the spectral
decompositions B = ∑3i=1 λi2 li ⊗ li and C = ∑3i=1 λi2 ri ⊗ ri , where {λi |i = 1, 2, 3} are the
principal stretches, {li |i = 1, 2, 3} are the principal directions of B, and {ri |i = 1, 2, 3} are
the principal directions of C. Finally, we introduce the logarithmic (Hencky) finite-strain
tensor in the deformed body: E = ∑3i=1 (ln λi )li ⊗ li .

principal stretches or three independent invariants. Stretch-based, Ogden-type free-energy
functions of the form ψ = ψ̌(λ1 , λ2 , λ3 ) are commonly used to model both incompressible

2.4     Validation experiments and simulations

In this section, we validate the predictive capability of our hyperelastic constitutive theory
by comparing model predictions against experimental measurements in settings involving
inhomogeneous deformation. To probe the model in a variety of situations, i.e., compression,
shear, and tension-dominated situations, we consider three specific types of validation tests:
(1) spherical and conical indentation, (2) simple-shear-like deformation both without and
with a fixed amount of pre-compression, and (3) tension of a specimen with circular holes.
The constitutive model of Section 2.3 has been numerically implemented in Abaqus/Standard
[61] using a user-material (UMAT) subroutine, and throughout this section, we obtain model
predictions in inhomogeneous deformation scenarios using finite-element calculations in
Abaqus/Standard.

2.6     Concluding remarks

Summary In this work, we utilized an extensive set of experiments on three densities of
the polyurethane-based, open-cell elastomeric foam “Poron XRD”. Experiments consisted
of homogeneous simple compression/tension as well as three types of inhomogeneous
experiments: spherical and conical indentation, simple-shear-like deformation without and
with pre-compression, and tension of a specimen with circular holes. A phenomenological,
isotropic, finite-deformation, hyperelastic constitutive model based on invariants of the
logarithmic, Hencky strain was proposed. A key simplifying assumption of our modeling
approach is that volumetric/distortional coupling only involves low-order dependence on the
magnitude of distortional deformation – which leads to a more straightforward interpretation
of the fitting functions and a systematic path for material parameter estimation from simple
compression/tension data (see Appendix A). In compression and tension, the fitted model for
each of the three densities of Poron XRD faithfully captures the nonlinear stress versus axial
strain and lateral strain versus axial strain responses, in particular, the tension/compression
asymmetry featuring a nearly-flat plateau regime in compression. The constitutive model
was implemented in Abaqus [61] using a user-material subroutine, which was used to obtain
model predictions in inhomogeneous deformation settings for the purpose of validation. The
model predictions were shown to be consistent with experimental data in the inhomogenous
validation cases across compression, shear, and tension-dominated settings. The user-
material subroutine implementation of the constitutive model and input files for each
validation case have been made available (https://github.com/HenannResearchGroup).


Limitations Our hyperelastic model was developed to capture the equilibrium response
of open-cell, isotropic elastomeric foams with relatively high void volume fraction. As such,
                                                                                           43
the model has several operational limitations arising from assumptions made in model devel-
opment. First, many elastomeric foam materials exhibit strain-rate-dependent, viscoelastic
material behavior, whereas the proposed model is rate-independent. Second, the polymeric
matrix material of the foam may be temperature sensitive, in some cases dramatically so, and
this effect may be coupled with the rate-sensitivity of the matrix material. All experiments
in the present work were conducted under approximately isothermal conditions at room
temperature. Accordingly, the model also assumes isothermal conditions, and the effect of
variable temperature is not captured. To use the model at elevated or reduced temperature, a
new set of experiments at the temperature of interest would be required. Third, depending
upon the microstructure, elastomeric foam materials may exhibit strain localization into
compaction bands under uniform external loading. The foam material considered in the
present work did not exhibit localization, and localization behavior is not accounted for in
the model.




Future work There are numerous avenues for future work. This study focused on a single
polyurethane-based, open-cell elastomeric foam material over a moderate range of densities.
We expect that the mechanical behavior of other open-cell foam materials may be well
captured by the proposed model, so long as the material does not exhibit discernible strain
localization. Future work will apply the proposed modeling approach to other elastomeric
foam materials. We expect that improvements will be made to the phenomenological
fitting functions utilized in the present work, either driven by additional experimental
data for different elastomeric foam materials or motivated by micromechanical arguments.
Furthermore, while the model has been tested in compression, shear, and tension-dominated
situations, model validation has not exhaustively spanned all combinations of the invariants
{K1 , K2 , K3 }. For example, it remains to test the proposed model in equibiaxial tension, in
which K1 > 0 and K3 = −1 (cf., e.g., the simple compression, K1 < 0 and K3 = −1, or simple
tension, K1 > 0 and K3 = 1, modes used in model calibration). Beyond hyperelastic modeling
44
of elastomeric foams, we envision that the proposed modeling framework may be extended
to incorporate the effects of strain-rate and temperature sensitivity by adapting existing finite
viscoelasticity frameworks for monolithic elastomers [e.g., 27, 68]. Incorporating the effects
of strain-rate sensitivity will be the focus of the subsequent chapters. Finally, localization
arising from microstructural defects is a key aspect of deformation in very-low-density
foams, and developing a model that captures this behavior will be the subject of future work.

3.1     Introduction

Due to the viscoelastic behavior of the matrix material, many elastomeric foams dis-
play a highly dissipative, time-dependent mechanical response, manifesting in strain-rate-
dependence and hysteresis under reversed loading. These materials are widely used in
situations in which both compliance and energy dissipation are necessary, e.g., impact
protection, cushioning, and vibration damping, and appear in a wide variety of specific
applications, such as equipment for impact protection. In order to best harness the dissipative
potential of this class of materials, a predictive constitutive model for their rate-dependent,
large-deformation mechanical behavior is needed for use in design.

   The complex mechanical behavior of viscoelastic elastomeric foams involves rate-
dependence, energy dissipation, large deformations, volumetric compressibility, and substan-
tial tension/compression asymmetry. The purpose of this chapter is to outline the framework
of a nonlinear viscoelastic constitutive model that is applicable to a wide variety of elas-
tomeric foams. We focus on the rate-dependent behavior of two types of viscoelastic foams:

     Most existing large-deformation nonlinear viscoelasticity models employ a decompo-
sition of the material response into two parts: a time-independent hyperelastic part and a
time-dependent viscoelastic part. For the hyperelastic part, rubber elasticity models are com-
monly employed using familiar material models, such as Ogden-type hyperelastic models
[e.g., 5, 27], invariant-based models [e.g., 25, 29], and other modified hyperelastic models
[e.g., 23, 30].

     Then, there are two prevalent approaches in the literature for formulating the time-
dependent part of a large-deformation nonlinear viscoelasticity model. The first is the
hereditary integral modeling approach, which is motivated by linear viscoelastic modeling.

Hereditary integral models are formulated in terms of relaxation or memory functions
[e.g., 5, 22–26]. Most nonlinear viscoelastic constitutive models of this type have been
experimentally calibrated and validated using only loading curves, and only a few works
deal with the unloading curves. For example, Ju et al. [25] modeled the viscoelastic
contribution to the stress using a hereditary integral model with different loading and
unloading parameters. Most hereditary-integral-based models utilize a Zener model form,
which is composed of a spring (rate-independent part) in parallel with a Maxwell element
(rate-dependent part). Briody et al. [5] and Yang and Shim [24] modelled the strain-rate
sensitivity of foam materials using a number of Maxwell elements with different relaxation
times. Several works consider a wide strain-rate range. For example, Yang and Shim [24]
characterized Poron XRD foams over the strain-rate range 1.0 × 10−2 -1.0 × 103 s−1 , and
Esposito et al. [26] modeled a closed-cell foam for strain rates spanning over six decades.
However, most hereditary-integral-based models are limited to a narrow strain-rate range.
In general, while the hereditary-integral-based modeling approach can capture nonlinear
viscoelastic behavior in compression, it is difficult to establish thermodynamic consistency,
54
and it is difficult to generalize the predictive capability of such models to arbitrary three-
dimensional loading histories.


     The second approach is based on the framework of the multiplicative decomposi-
tion of the deformation gradient into elastic and inelastic parts. Nearly all nonlinear
viscoelastic models that follow this approach have been applied to incompressible or nearly-
incompressible materials [e.g., 27–33], and few researchers have reported on the modelling
of highly-compressible foam materials [e.g., 34]. Within this model structure, Zener-type
models [e.g., 27] and models involving an arbitrary number of parallel Maxwell elements
[e.g., 70] are widely used. Like existing hereditary-integral-based models, only several
works [e.g., 32] have described the rate-dependent viscoelastic behavior of elastomeric
materials over a wide strain-rate range (e.g., 10 × 10−3 -10 × 101 s−1 for polyurea in Shim
and Mohr [32]), and most multiplicative-decomposition-based models have only been ex-
perimentally validated for narrow ranges of strain-rates. Compared to hereditary integral
models, the multiplicative-decomposition-based approach leads to models that are thermo-
dynamically consistent and may be straightforwardly applied to arbitrary three-dimensional
loading histories.


     The present work focuses on the modeling of the loading and unloading response of
highly compressible elastomeric foams over a wide range of strain-rates from 1.0 × 10−3 s−1
to 1.0 × 101 s−1 and at large compressive strain magnitudes (up to 0.9 true strain). This
chapter lays out a general modeling framework that is intended for a broad class of foam
materials. The model framework consists of a time-independent, hyperelastic contribution
and several time-dependent, viscoelastic contributions. The equilibrium hyperelastic part
is based on the model of Chapter 2. For the rate-dependent mechanisms, we employ the
rheological concept of parallel Maxwell elements and the framework of the multiplicative
decomposition of the deformation gradient into non-equilibrium elastic and viscous parts.
In this chapter, the modeling framework is left in a general form that may then be applied to
                                                                                                                  55
different specific foam materials to capture the different behaviors demonstrated in Figs. 3.1-
3.4. Then, in Chapters 4 and 5, the framework will be specialized to the closed-cell EVA
foam and the open-cell Poron XRD foams, respectively.



3.2      Constitutive theory

In this section, we describe our isotropic, viscoelastic model for nonlinear, finite-deformation,
rate-dependent behavior of elastomeric foams. Our constitutive modeling approach is based
upon a decomposition of the response into a hyperelastic, equilibrium response and a series
of dissipative, non-equilibrium contributions. For conceptual clarity, this approach may be
thought of as a nonlinear and finite-deformation generalization of the Maxwell-Wiechert
model of linear viscoelasticity, shown schematically in Fig. 3.7.1 With reference to the
schematic, the left branch represents the equilibrium, hyperelastic response of the material,
which is described by the model introduced in Chapter 2 and recapped in Section 3.2.1.
Then, the non-equilibrium, time-dependent material response is described by N nonlinear,
Maxwell-like, non-equilibrium mechanisms in parallel with the equilibrium branch. Each
mechanism – denoted by α – is described by the model discussed in Section 3.2.2.

decomposition-based strategy. We expect that this modeling approach may be applied
to a variety of foam materials so long as the material does not exhibit discernible strain
localization. In Chapter 4 and Chapter 5, we apply the model to closed-cell EVA foam and

4.4      Concluding remarks

In this chapter, we applied the model proposed in Chapter 3 to the closed-cell EVA foam
over strain-rate range 10−3 -101 s−1 . The fitted model captures the observed rate-dependence
of the engineering stress/strain response, including hysteretic behavior upon unloading,
asymmetry in compression/tension, and the lateral strain versus axial strain response. The
proposed constitutive theory is able to accommodate the changes in rate-dependence with
increasing strain rate, i.e., the foam material we focus on in this chapter is less rate-dependent
                                                                                          79
for strain rates below 101 s−1 than for strain rates above. We demonstrated that the model is
able to capture the response in inhomogenous validation cases across compression, shear,
and tension-dominated settings and at various deformation rates.

5.3     Validation experiments and simulations

The constitutive model of Section 5.2 has been numerically implemented in Abaqus/Explicit
[61] using a user-material (VUMAT) subroutine. To probe the predictive capability of
our viscoelastic model in various situations involving compression, shear, and tension-
dominated deformation, a series of experiments that involve inhomogeneous deformation
have been carried out. The model predictions obtained using finite-element calculations in
Abaqus/Explicit are compared against the responses measured from experiments. Three
specific validation tests are done: (1) conical indentation, (2) simple-shear-like deformation

circular holes. For all validation tests, we use displacement control, and the displacement
loading profile is chosen based on two criteria: (1) The local strain-rate fields achieved in
the validation tests should remain within the fitted strain-rate range everywhere. (2) We aim
to probe a wide strain-rate range.

5.4      Concluding remarks

Summary In this chapter, we applied the viscoelastic model proposed in Chapter 3 to
open-cell Poron XRD foams of three densities over the strain-rate range 10−3 -100 s−1 . The
fitted model captures the observed rate-dependence of the engineering stress/strain response,
including hysteretic behavior upon unloading, asymmetry in compression/tension, as well
as the lateral strain versus axial strain response. Particularly, the calibrated constitutive
theory is able to accommodate the sharpening of the transition from the linear regime to the
plateau regime and the earlier transition from the plateau regime to the stiffening regime as
the strain-rate increases for the Poron XRD foams.

   The fitted model has been validated against a comprehensive set of inhomogeneous
deformation experiments. The experiments consist of conical indentation, simple-shear-like

Limitations and future work In this chapter, we have focused on a single polyurethane-

Conclusions and outlook


6.1     Concluding remarks

Elastomeric foams are an important class of engineering materials with wide applications
that pose significant challenges in constitutive modeling. The current predictive capability
for these materials – especially in the large-deformation regime – lags behind other classes
of engineering materials, such as metals, plastics, and homogeneous elastomers. The work
in this thesis addressed this limitation and proposed a large-deformation, viscoelastic consti-
tutive modeling framework for elastomeric foams. The main conclusions and contributions
of this thesis are summarized as follows:


   • A phenomenological, isotropic, hyperelastic constitutive model based on invariants of
      the logarithmic, Hencky strain was proposed for the quasi-static, large-deformation
      mechanical behavior of elastomeric foams. We applied the model to three densities of
      the polyurethane-based, open-cell elastomeric foam “Poron XRD.” The model was
      fitted to homogeneous simple compression/tension experimental data and validated
      against three types of inhomogeneous experiments: spherical and conical indentation,
      simple-shear-like deformation without and with pre-compression, and tension of a
      specimen with circular holes. The model predictions were shown to be consistent
                                             103
104
      with experimental data in the inhomogeneous validation cases. A key simplifying
      assumption of our modeling approach is that volumetric/distortional coupling only
      involves low-order dependence on the magnitude of distortional deformation – which
      leads to a more straightforward interpretation of the fitting functions and a systematic
      path for material parameter estimation from simple compression/tension data. Since
      most of the material parameters are density-independent, we may straightforwardly
      apply the model to a foam made of the same matrix material but of different densities.


  • A phenomenological, isotropic, viscoelastic constitutive model based on a multi-
      mechanism, multiplicative-decomposition based strategy was proposed for the time-
      dependent, large-deformation mechanical behavior of elastomeric foams. A key
      simplifying assumption of our modeling approach is that the non-equilibrium free-
      energy density function has the same functional form as the equilibrium free-energy
      density function, i.e., the hyperelastic model discussed above. Another key simplifying
      assumption is that for the flow rule, we constrain the number of non-Newtonian, shear
      thinning branches to be one or zero, and the remaining branches are Newtonian, which
      allows a more straightforward interpretation of the fitting parameters for the flow rule
      and a reduced number of adjustable material parameters. By invoking only one non-
      Newtonian, shear thinning branch and allowing the equilibrium free-energy function
      and the non-equilibrium free-energy function to have different material parameters, we
      have enabled the viscoelastic constitutive model to be flexible enough to accommodate
      different rate-dependent behaviors.


  • We applied the proposed viscoelastic constitutive model to two different types of
      foam material - a closed-cell foam and several densities of an open-cell foam – which
      exhibit notably different rate-dependent behaviors. For both materials, the viscoelas-
      tic model was fitted to homogeneous simple compression/tension experimental data
      over quasi-static and moderate strain-rate ranges. As for the hyperelastic model,

Determining whether a shear thinning branch is necessary First, we determine whether
a shear thinning branch is necessary. To reduce model complexity, in application, we con-
strain the number of shear thinning branches to be at most one. We then examine plots
of the amount of hysteresis D (defined in (3.2)) and the normalized average stress-level
difference σ̄d 1 as a function of strain rate. Figure D.2(b) shows how the hysteresis-amount
changes with strain rate, and Figs. D.2(c) and (d) show how the average stress-level and
the normalized average stress-level difference vary as a function of strain rate, respectively.
It is expected that the amount of hysteresis should increase as a function of strain rate. In
the present case, D shows slight fluctuations over the first three strain rates, but we regard
D as nearly uniform over this strain-rate range given the fact that the maximum strain
magnitude in compression varies slightly between experiments. Overall, here we have that
the amount of hysteresis is nearly uniform before increasing slowly with strain rate. In the
case of a monotonically-increasing amount of hysteresis, we propose a straightforward way
to determine whether a shear thinning branch is required. We compare experimental data for
the normalized average stress-level difference as a function of strain rate (blue symbols in
Fig. D.2(d)) to the heuristic criterion shown as green symbols in Fig. D.2(d). The criterion
we adopt is if the experimental data is below 60% of the constant Gneq case (discussed
further below) shown as black symbols in Fig. D.2(d), this indicates that a shear thinning
branch is required, which is the case for the experimental data considered here.
